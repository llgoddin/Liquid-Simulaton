# SPH solver formulation

`Particle_Manager` uses a two-dimensional weakly-compressible SPH solver; the application enables it independently of the mouse-force control. The 2D poly6 density kernel, spiky pressure gradient, and nonnegative 2D viscosity Laplacian are normalized over a disk of support radius `h`; density includes self contribution. The linear equation of state is `p=max(0,k*(rho-rho0))`, an explicit free-surface negative-pressure clamp.

Pressure uses the symmetric Becker–Teschner conservative pair-force equation `-mi*mj*(pi/rhoi² + pj/rhoj²)*grad(W)`; viscosity uses `mu*mi*mj/(rhoi*rhoj)*(vj-vi)*L`. These are forces, accumulated equal and opposite, not Müller force-density equations or Becker acceleration equations. The 2D kernels follow David Bindel, *Deriving SPH*, §§2–3 (https://www.cs.cornell.edu/~bindel/class/cs5220-f11/code/sph-derive.pdf); the symmetric pressure form follows Becker and Teschner, *Weakly compressible SPH for free surface flows*, §3 (https://cg.informatik.uni-freiburg.de/publications/2007_SCA_SPH.pdf). At distinct coincident positions the undefined pressure direction is zero; viscosity remains active.

The solver computes densities, pressures and pair forces from one pre-move position/velocity snapshot. `Particle.move` scales forces by `dt * 120` for compatibility with the existing mouse-force convention. SPH pair forces and gravity are physical forces, so the manager divides them by 120 exactly once before applying them. Gravity is 10 px/s²; the former `-10` force per unit mass acted as a 10-unit velocity impulse each 1/120-second step, equivalent to 1,200 px/s². The gravity toggle is therefore intentionally milder than the earlier implementation. Mouse interaction remains on its historical reference-rate scale.

The manager creates reflected ghost samples for particles within `h` of each container plane and the circular obstacle. Ghosts mirror position across the plane or radially across the obstacle's particle-radius-aware contact surface, and reflect the wall-normal velocity component. At corners it also adds the combined image. For the circle, the ghost mass is weighted by `(R_contact - gap) / (R_contact + gap)`, the 2D polar-area Jacobian for radial reflection. This is a local approximate boundary condition, not a published general curved-wall method; circular ghost samples are limited to `gap < min(h, R_contact)` to avoid reflecting through the center. The obstacle collision projection remains the non-penetration safeguard. The force, speed and timestep limits are broad runaway guards, not SPH calibration or stability criteria.

## Measured evidence and unresolved limits

The tests cover normalized-kernel checks, planar and circular density probes, reflected velocity, circular-geometry translation/rotation invariance, a deterministic 200-particle one-second stress case, timestep refinement, and a gravity-loaded column. In a planar lattice with `h=30`, pitch 10, and unit masses, near-wall/interior density was 0.693901 without support and 0.950629 with one equal-mass static wall row 10 px behind the wall; the mirrored-sample unit test recovers a ratio of 1.000000 for its matching lattice. This is a planar result, not a validation for arbitrary walls.

For the project's default circular geometry (`R=35`, particle radius 5, `h=30`, pitch 15), the reproducible probe `SDL_VIDEODRIVER=dummy SDL_AUDIODRIVER=dummy .env/bin/python scripts/qualify_sph_lattices.py` measured near/interior density ratios of 0.778108→1.058332 on a symmetric radial ring and 0.785539→1.062826 on a full square-lattice annulus (with/without ghosts). On the radial ring, mean near-wall radial force changed from −46.1402 (toward the obstacle) to −1.9895; on the square annulus it changed from −0.8201 to +9.7852. Density support is within 6.3% of the interior reference for these static default-scale samples, but radial force still varies by sample layout; this does not establish a universal curved-wall force model.

The 164-particle default-scale annulus also passes a translation/rotation invariance test (center moved by `(250,-180)`, whole scene rotated 37°): density changes by less than `1e-12` and rotated force components by less than `1e-10`. The reproducible dynamic comparison is `SDL_VIDEODRIVER=dummy SDL_AUDIODRIVER=dummy .env/bin/python scripts/qualify_sph_walls.py --seconds 5`; it drops 200 particles onto a radius-35 obstacle for 600 steps, with container ghosts retained in both arms. With circle ghosts, the 21 particles within `h` of contact had mean density 1.0474× rest density (range 0.7992–1.2031); without circle ghosts, 23 near-wall particles averaged 1.0095× (range 0.7216–1.2109). Both runs stayed finite, had zero clamp activations and maximum speed 22.325 px/s; maximum obstacle penetration was 0 px with ghosts and 7.1e-15 px without (floating-point round-off). The ghosts raised the low-density tail but also raised the mean, so this run is mixed evidence, not a general wall-support pass.

A gravity-loaded 80-particle column (pitch 15, 10 by 8 initial grid, bottom at `y=-100`) was run for 5 simulated seconds. Mean pressure by height layer, bottom to top, was 1.964, 1.282, 0.557 and 0.207; no velocity clamp activated. Replaying its initial state for 2 seconds at `dt=1/120` and `dt=1/240` gave a maximum particle-position difference of 0.0418 px and maximum velocity difference of 0.0401 px/s.

The reproducible long-run command is `SDL_VIDEODRIVER=dummy SDL_AUDIODRIVER=dummy .env/bin/python scripts/qualify_sph.py --seconds 60`. It exercises a deterministic 200-particle constructor lattice of radius-5 particles, gravity and SPH on, bounds `±500 × ±400`, an obstacle of radius 35 at the origin, and 7,200 fixed steps. The runner exits nonzero unless state remains finite, speed stays below its cap, obstacle penetration is at most `1e-9` px, and all clamp counters remain zero. After the obstacle setup changes, a fresh run remained finite; density ranged from 0.3183 to 2.4598 times rest density, maximum speed was 42.732 px/s, obstacle penetration was 0, and force/velocity/timestep clamp counters all remained zero. It took 54.78 wall seconds (7.61 ms per physics step). This physics-only run is not a full-application frame-rate measurement. These results support bounded behavior and modest timestep sensitivity in the tested cases, but do not establish general physical accuracy or universal curved-wall support; the wall response is only qualified for the documented cases.
